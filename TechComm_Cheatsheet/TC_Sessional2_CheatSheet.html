Meetings: the 3 aspects ★★

A meeting always has a purpose (give information, take decisions). A good meeting needs careful preparation. The 3 important aspects of a meeting are Notice → Agenda → Minutes: before, during, after.

4 Ws of a successful meeting: Why, Who, Where, When.

1. NOTICE ★★★

Definition: A notice is written or printed information, usually displayed publicly, that conveys the same message to many people at once. For a meeting, it is the information sent to all participants telling them a meeting is being called (convened).

Purpose: to inform, advertise, instruct, warn. Shown at railway stations, bus stands, hospitals, colleges, govt. offices. Sent by printed circular, e-mail or phone.

Qualities of a good notice

  • Accurate, clear, concise, decisive
  • Correct, unambiguous, polite language; short words and sentences
  • Attractive layout; reader-friendly; readable from a distance

A notice must contain

  • Title / heading ("NOTICE"), name of organisation
  • Date of issue
  • Event information + type of meeting (committee, AGM…)
  • Date, time, venue of the event
  • Purpose (agenda) of the meeting
  • Name + designation + signature of the issuer
  • Copies to (Cc) at the bottom left/right

Drafting tips: use organisation's letterhead; send in reasonable time (generally 2 weeks before); put it in a box.

ABC INSTITUTE, GREATER NOIDA NOTICE 13 Oct 2026 BLOOD DONATION CAMP All members are informed that a blood donation camp will be held on 20 Oct 2026, 10 am–4 pm, at the Main Auditorium. Interested students may register with the undersigned by 17 Oct. Sd/- Rahul Verma, Secretary, NSS Club Cc: Director, HoDs

2. AGENDA ★★★

Definition: An agenda is the official list of items (topics) to be discussed or dealt with at a meeting, arranged in order. From Latin agendum = "a thing to be done". It outlines the business of a forthcoming meeting and is usually sent along with the notice.

Who prepares: the Secretary, in consultation with the Chairperson; signed by the Secretary; distributed before the meeting. Written in future tense.

Format / order of items

  1. First item always: Confirmation of minutes of the previous meeting
  2. Matters arising from those minutes (status of pending work)
  3. Main issues, in order of priority
  4. Any other business (with Chair's permission)
  5. Last item: Date of the next meeting

Should include: location, date, timing, topics + short description, time given to each topic, names of members.

Language: Brevity, Clarity, Informativeness, Relevance.

Writing steps: list attendees → list topics → note purpose of each (information / brainstorming / decision) → drop irrelevant items → link people to topics → check against meeting objectives.

Advantages: confirms date/time/place; directs the course of meeting; prevents digressions; gives members time to prepare opinions; helps reach consensus. Without it a meeting is a waste of time.

3. MINUTES OF MEETING ★★★

Definition: Minutes are the official written record of the discussions held and decisions/resolutions taken at a meeting, in chronological order. They are concise and written after the meeting in past tense.

Written by the Secretary; signed by Secretary + Chairperson (after approval by members).

Minutes must give

  1. Name of organisation/unit
  2. Day, date, time, place
  3. Serial no. (e.g., "33rd meeting of…")
  4. Names of Chairperson & Secretary
  5. Members present and absentees (leave of absence)
  6. Record of each item: discussion + decision taken + action by whom
  7. Signatures

Importance: aid to memory; basis for action; informs absent members; legal/official record.

4. JOB APPLICATION (Cover Letter) ★★★

Definition: A job application letter (also called a cover letter) is a formal letter sent with the résumé. The résumé lists your history; the letter explains why you are qualified and should be called for an interview. It sets the tone of the résumé.

Basic structure (3 parts): Introduction (post applied for + where you saw the advert) → Details (qualifications, experience, how you meet their needs; the most important part) → Conclusion (thank them, request an interview, follow-up info).

Elements

  • Heading: your + employer's contact details, date
  • Subject: Application for the post of …
  • Salutation: Dear Mr./Ms. + last name / Dear Sir/Madam
  • Body: 3 paragraphs above
  • Complimentary close: Yours sincerely / Sincerely
  • Signature + typed name; Enclosure: Résumé

Exam tip: "Job application" = cover letter + résumé (2 parts).

5. CV vs RÉSUMÉ ★★★

Résumé (French word = "summary"): a short (1 page, max 2) tool to market your skills, experience, education and achievements, tailored to one job. Used for any job. Starts with Summary of Qualifications → Areas of strength (keywords) → Experience in reverse order.

CV / Curriculum Vitae (Latin = "course of life"): a long, detailed record of your entire academic and professional history, including degrees, publications, research, affiliations. Used for academic, scientific, research posts (and generally in Europe/Asia).

Résumé contents (in order) ★★★

  1. Heading: full name, complete address, phone (C/H/W), professional e-mail (not "rambocecil@…")
  2. Objective / Profile summary (optional): specific & concise.
    ✔ "An internship at Computer Sciences Corporation in computer programming."
    ✘ "An internship utilizing my skills and training."
  3. Education: reverse chronology; institution, location, year, degree, major/minor, CGPA, awards, study abroad
  4. Related coursework (optional): when lacking experience
  5. Experience: jobs, internships, volunteer, projects; can split into Related / Additional
  6. Skills: computer (languages: C++, Java; apps; OS: Unix/Linux) + languages with proficiency
  7. Honours, activities, research, professional affiliations
  8. Achievements, co-/extra-curricular, strengths, hobbies, references, personal details

✘ Never include religious affiliation.

Résumé style & writing tips ★★

  • Font 10–12 pt, Times New Roman/Arial; ½"–1" margins; balance white space
  • Bold/CAPS for headings; lines between sections; bullets with action verbs; quantify results
  • White/light paper; same paper for résumé, cover letter, references
  • Customise for each job; accentuate the positive; employers glance only 10–20 seconds
  • Always send a cover letter; paper clip, don't staple

3 C's of résumé: Clarity (clear, concise presentation), Consistency (don't mix categories/dates), Conciseness (summarise, highlight).

ATS-friendly résumé ★

ATS = Applicant Tracking Software/System: software that scans résumés for keywords before a human sees them. Use simple formatting (no tables/graphics), keywords from the job description, standard fonts (Arial/Calibri), bullet points, standard headings (Work Experience, Education, Skills).

7 C's of Effective Communication ★★★

  1. Completeness: gives all information the audience needs; leaves no questions.
  2. Conciseness: least possible words without losing meaning ("Brevity is the soul of wit"); no unnecessary details.
  3. Consideration: "stepping into the shoes of others": the "You" attitude; knowing reader's background, mindset, education.
  4. Clarity: one specific goal at a time; familiar words, avoid jargon.
  5. Concreteness: specific and particular, not vague/general; exact facts and figures.
  6. Courtesy: sincere, polite, judicious, enthusiastic; builds goodwill.
  7. Correctness: correct grammar, format, content and facts.

REPORT: definition & purpose ★★★

Definition: From Latin "reportare" = to carry back. A report is a formal, factual document, based on evidence, written for a specific audience to meet a specific need. It presents facts about a situation/project/process, analyses and interprets them, draws conclusions and gives recommendations. It is a basic management tool for decision-making. Length: 1-page trip report to 250-page annual report; oral, written or electronic.

Purposes/significance: present data; record events; describe problems + suggest solutions; analyse a situation; give feedback/recommendations; standard reference for planning; easy circulation of information.

Characteristics of a good report ★★★

  • Precision: clear purpose → unity and coherence
  • Factual details: accurate, detailed facts
  • Relevance: only relevant facts (irrelevant ones confuse)
  • Reader-oriented: written keeping the reader in mind
  • Simple, unambiguous language: clear, brief, grammatical
  • Special format: cover, title page, contents, illustrations, appendices
  • Homogeneity: deals with one main topic
  • Documentation: sources acknowledged properly
  • Also: objectivity, clarity, accuracy (ambiguity is NOT a feature)

4 Formats of a report ★★★

Chosen by nature, scope, length, function, audience.

  1. Pre-printed form: fill-in/tick form for routine info (daily production). Systematic, objective, saves time.
  2. Letter format: short report to someone outside the organisation (external). Letter parts + headings, references.
  3. Memo format: short report within the organisation (internal). To/From/Date/Subject + headings.
  4. Manuscript format: long, formal reports (few to hundreds of pages), sections with headings. Longest format.

"Demi-official" is NOT a report format.

Structure of a FORMAL (long) report ★★★

A. Prefatory / Front matter

  • Title page: title, submitted to, submitted by, date (report no., logo)
  • Preface (optional, only in published reports): writer's personal intro: background, reasons, scope, highlights
  • Letter of transmittal: covering letter stating purpose, scope, highlights
  • Acknowledgement: "thank-you note" to contributors
  • Table of contents: headings/sub-headings + page numbers
  • List of illustrations: tables, graphs, figures
  • Abstract / Executive summary: gist of key findings, conclusions, recommendations

B. Main body

  • Introduction: background, aims, scope & limitations; proposes the problem
  • Methodology: how data was collected (library, internet, interviews, surveys)
  • Discussion/Findings/Analysis: longest, main part; organised data with headings
  • Conclusion: summary of findings and their meaning
  • Recommendations: suggested course of action, further work

C. Supplementary / Back matter

  • Appendices: supporting material (questionnaires, charts) kept separate so body flows
  • References: sources cited in text · Bibliography: sources consulted, not cited (alphabetical)
  • Glossary: technical terms + meanings, alphabetical ("dictionary")
  • Index: alphabetical topics with page nos.; for bulky reports

Short report ★★

½ to 2 pages, in memo-like format, to inform an authority on one matter for a decision. Parts: Introduction → Discussion → Summary → Conclusions → Recommendations → Appendix.

Steps of writing a report / research paper ★★

Analyse problem/purpose (Terms of Reference) → Scope → Audience analysis → Investigation (primary & secondary sources) → Organise → Outline → First draft → Revise, review, edit → Final draft

Project report & Thesis/Dissertation ★★★

Project report: a completion report written after finishing a project. Gives an account of its stages, and a systematic presentation and discussion of data collected and analysed. Internal or external.

Thesis/Dissertation: a long research report presenting original research (or data from sources), describing and analysing a systematic investigation to add to knowledge in a field. It covers what was known before, progress made, interpretation, and future scope.

Extra elements beyond a formal report: Background, Objectives, Literature Review, Materials & Equipment, Theory & Procedure.

TECHNICAL PROPOSAL ★★★

Definition: "Propose" = "to put forward for consideration". A proposal is a systematic, formal and persuasive set of recommendations to solve a problem, written for a specific audience before a project begins. It explains and justifies what it proposes; it is the first step of a new project.

Uses: initiate new projects; fresh ideas; solve problems; promote innovative strategies; win contracts. Importance: records of info, index of company growth, financial gain, promote research.

Types

  • By subject: Business/Sales (external, to clients), Research (academic), Technical
  • Solicited: written in response to a request/invitation (RFP, advert)
  • Unsolicited: on writer's own initiative, no request
  • Internal (within org.) / External (outside) · Formal (long) / Informal (short)

Writing steps: Planning (observe, methods) → Collect data (observation, interviews, questionnaire; internal records, library, online) → Write (rough draft → proofread → final).

Structure of a formal proposal ★★★

Prefatory: Title page · Letter of transmittal · Draft contract (unique to proposals: rough terms/rules of work) · Contents · List of illustrations · Executive summary/Abstract

Main body:

  • Introduction: purpose, scope, limitations, problem, need, background; persuades
  • Technical section: methods, plan of action, solutions available and why one is chosen
  • Managerial section: sequence of activities (schedule); equipment, facilities, products; personnel qualifications
  • Cost estimate / Budget: mandatory; materials, salaries, travel, etc.
  • Conclusion: key benefits; final appeal

Supplementary: Appendices · References

TECHNICAL / SCIENTIFIC PAPER ★★

Definition: A written composition that describes, discusses and analyses a systematic investigation to add to knowledge in a specific field. It is documented, objective prose.

Features: adds to existing knowledge; transfers new findings to other researchers; systematic account of survey/research/field work; objective analysis of findings.

Characteristics: expository; highly stylised; objective, accurate, concise, unambiguous; uses illustrations & jargon; every claim backed by evidence.

Parts: Title → Author by-line → Abstract → Introduction → Methodology → Results → Discussion → Conclusion → Appendices → References. Title = long phrase with keywords: informative, specific.

Research methods: library research, electronic search, primary sources (surveys, questionnaires), experiments.

ETHICAL WRITING ★★

Definition: Writing that clearly shows (through documentation) where source material is used, uses correct and authentic data, and is inclusive and respectful of diversity. It avoids plagiarism, bias, and exclusive language (sexist, racist…), which increases the writer's credibility.

Citing rules: use latest style manual; cite every borrowed idea; cite both paraphrases and direct quotes.

PLAGIARISM ★★★

Definition: Presenting someone else's work, ideas, words, images or results as your own without acknowledgement. Merriam-Webster: "to steal and pass off the ideas or words of another as one's own"; literary theft.

Counts as plagiarism: submitting another's work; copying without credit; no quotation marks on a quote; wrong source info; changing words but keeping the sentence structure; copying so much it forms most of your work (even with credit).

Ways/types of plagiarising

  • Direct copying: copy text/ideas as-is and label them yours
  • Paraphrasing (without citation): change the language, keep the idea
  • Word switching: change only a few words of the original
  • Self-plagiarism: reuse your own published work as new

Law: Copyright Act, 1957

  • Sec 57: author's special/moral rights: claim authorship; claim damages for distortion/mutilation
  • Sec 63: 1st offence: 6 months–3 years jail + fine ₹50,000–₹2 lakh
  • Sec 63(a): 2nd offence: 1–3 years + ₹1–2 lakh

Avoid by: citing, quoting, referencing; reliable sources; plagiarism checkers.

COPY EDITING ★★

Definition: Revising written material before publication to improve readability and make it free of errors of punctuation, spelling, grammar, style and fact.

Copy editor's duties: fix grammar (spelling, tense, subject-verb agreement); check facts & accuracy; make verbose copy concise; write headlines; delete opinions/libellous statements.

Types: Proofreading (grammatical accuracy, final check) · Line editing (content, style, language per line) · Fact-checking (accuracy, ideally before copy-edit) · Rewriting (reword/reorganise for flow).

5 C's of editing: Clarity, Cogency, Conventionality, Completeness, Concision.

Copy editing = whole improvement; proofreading = final surface-error check.

REFERENCING ★★★

Definition: A standardised way of acknowledging the sources (books, journals, websites, theses…) of ideas, data, images used in your writing, so the sources can be identified and verified.

Why: prevents plagiarism; credits author; credibility; readers can verify/follow up.

2 parts: Citation (in-text): brief pointer in the text, e.g. [1] or (Smith, 2009) · Reference list: full details at the end.

References vs Bibliography: references = only sources cited; bibliography = all sources consulted (alphabetical).

Styles: IEEE (engineering/CS), APA (social science; 7th ed.), MLA (humanities), Chicago, Harvard, Oxford. MLA = Modern Language Association; APA = American Psychological Association.

Missing info: n.a. (no author), n.p. (no publisher), n.d. (no date).

APA (Author, Year, Title, Publisher; alphabetical) Raman, M. & Sharma, S. (2009). Professional communication. New Delhi: Oxford University Press. MLA (Name. Title. Place: Publisher, Year. Medium) Raman, Meenakshi and Sharma, Sangeeta. Professional Communication. New Delhi: OUP, 2009. Print. IEEE (numbered [1] in order of appearance) [1] S. Russell and P. Norvig, Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach, 4th ed. Pearson, 2021. [2] A. Sharma, "Title," IEEE Access, vol. 12, pp. 1234–1245, 2024.

Tools: Zotero, Mendeley, EndNote, Google Scholar, BibTeX. Figures/tables taken from others must cite source: "Fig. 1 … [6]".

Keynote speech ★

A speech with a main underlying theme, given at conventions/conferences to motivate and inspire (like persuasive speech). Use stories, humour, and repeat a core line to make the idea memorable.
